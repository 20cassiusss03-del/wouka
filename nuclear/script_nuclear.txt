A nuclear plant like the ones built across America in the 1970s makes electricity with two separate loops of water.
The first loop runs straight through the reactor core, picks up heat, and carries along anything radioactive the water can drag with it.
That water flows into the steam generator, a steel tank several stories tall packed with thousands of thin tubes, and it heats a second loop of clean water that turns to steam and spins the turbine.
The reactor water enters at the bottom, into a curved chamber the industry calls the channel head, and the workers call the bowl.
Over months of running, tiny bits of metal worn off the pipes ride through the core, turn radioactive, and settle on the walls of that bowl.
The worst of them is cobalt-60, which keeps throwing off gamma rays for years after the reactor is switched off.
So every time the plant shuts down for refueling, usually every eighteen months to two years, that bowl is one of the hottest places a person is legally allowed to go.
And during that shutdown, somebody has to go there.
The crew wants to flood the pool above the reactor so it can move fuel underwater, and at the same time it wants to open up the steam generator to inspect its tubes.
To do both at once, the big pipes between the reactor and the steam generator have to be sealed from the inside with plugs called nozzle dams.
Today, robots and remote tools handle a lot of that job.
For decades, a man in a plastic suit did it with his own hands.

Every worker at a nuclear plant wears a dosimeter, and every bit of radiation it records goes into a file with their name on it.
Before 1994, federal rules allowed three rem in any three-month stretch, with a lifetime cap tied to your age.
Since 1994, the cap has been five rem in a year.
Five rem is fifty millisieverts, and the average American gets about six millisieverts a year from everything combined.
A chest X-ray is about two hundredths of a millisievert.
So one year at the legal limit adds up to roughly eight years of ordinary life, or around two and a half thousand chest X-rays.

By the late 1970s, American nuclear plants were bringing in temporary workers by the thousands.
They mopped up spills, scrubbed contaminated floors, hunted for leaks, welded pipe and hauled radioactive waste in drums.
The regular staff had names for them: glow boys, gamma sponges, nuclear nomads.
In the early eighties, a freelance journalist named Paul Bagne went through the federal numbers and found something that didn't add up.
Between 1978 and 1980, the electricity coming out of American nuclear plants actually went down, while the number of workers exposed to radiation went from forty-four thousand to seventy-seven thousand.
Nearly half of them were temporary.
The plants were making less power and spreading the radiation across far more bodies to do it.
Critics at the time claimed that some of these men moved from plant to plant, and when they hit the limit at one, they signed on at the next.

One account in a California paper put it in plain words: "They do it because it pays well. Maybe they make a jump and then have some extra money for a vacation that year, or maybe it helps them through rough times in between jobs."
For some men that meant a trip somewhere warm, and for others it meant rent in the gap between two jobs.
